## Regression and correlation

This unit deals with describing relationships between two quantitative variables, e.g. age and income.

**Correlation** answers the following question: is there really a relationship between the two variables?

**Regression** answers the following question: how do we predict values for one variable, given particular values for another variable(s)?

## Regression analysis

Regression analysis models situations in which there is (at least) one dependent variable and at least one independent variable.

For this course we will only be modelling with ONE dependent and ONE independent variable. As an example, consider the variables years of education and income. Suppose we are hypothesising that an individual's income is determined (to some degree) by their years of education. Thus we are proposing that "income" is the dependent variable and "years of education" is the independent variable. We are stating a belief that (current) income depends on the number of years of education that an individual can claim credit for. Note that the converse is not necessarily true. We will employ regression analysis to predict an individual's income from their years of education.

## Scatterplots

Regression analysis is only effective as a predictive tool if a relationship actually exists between the dependent and independent variables. Scatterplots can be used as a preliminary method for identifying relationships between two variables. We are interested in categorising the relationships between two variables as:

- **positive**: as X increases, so does Y;
- **negative**: as X increases, Y decreases; or
- **no relationship**.

The relationship could also be linear or non-linear. In this course we will only be dealing with linear regression, i.e. a relationship in which the dependent variable is a linear function of the independent variable.

## Building the regression model

We attempt to fit a straight line (since we are dealing with linear regression) through our observed data. The regression equation is of the form

$$
Y = a + bX,
$$

where $Y$ is the dependent variable and $X$ is the independent variable. $a$ and $b$ are chosen so that the regression line "best fits" the observed data. We need to calculate $a$ and $b$: $a$ is the **intercept** term and $b$ is the **slope**.

How do we determine the best regression line to use? How do we determine $a$ and $b$?

## Method of least squares

The constants $a$ and $b$ are chosen to minimise the sum of squared residuals.

<Definition term="Residual">
A residual ($e$) is the difference between the actual $y$ and $\hat{y}$, the predicted value of $y$:

$$
e = y - \hat{y}.
$$

If you have $n$ pairs of observations then you'll have $n$ residuals.
</Definition>

$$
\text{Sum of squared residuals} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2.
$$

We want to minimise this! The line that does so is the **least-squares regression line**.

## Correlation analysis

Correlation is a measure of the linear relationship between X and Y. It is a numerical measure of the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables. If we have access to all individuals in a population, we can calculate the correlation for the population (the true correlation value); otherwise we would calculate the sample correlation.

The sample correlation coefficient ($r$) can be used to justify the existence of a relationship between X and Y, and hence the usefulness of a particular regression analysis.

<Definition term="Sample correlation coefficient">
$$
r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{(n - 1)\, s_x s_y}
$$

The correlation coefficient always lies between $-1$ and $1$.
</Definition>

- $r > 0$: a positive linear relationship; the points rise from left to right.
- $r = +1$: a perfect positive linear relationship; every point lies on a rising straight line.
- $r < 0$: a negative linear relationship; the points fall from left to right.
- $r = -1$: a perfect negative linear relationship; every point lies on a falling straight line.
- $r \approx 0$: no linear relationship.

## Coefficient of determination

The square of the correlation coefficient, $R^2$, is known as the **coefficient of determination**. It is a useful measure which indicates the proportion of the variation in Y that can be explained by the variation in X. In Excel's output it is the **R Square**.

$R^2$ lies between 0 and 1. The closer $R^2$ is to 1, the better the regression model fits the data. The closer it is to 0, the poorer the fit. If $r$ is close to $+1$ or $-1$, $R^2$ will be close to 1, and if $r$ is close to 0, $R^2$ will also be close to 0.

For example, if $R^2 = 0.8697$, then this is interpreted as: 86.97% of the variation in Y is explained by the variation in X. Since this is a large proportion, we would conclude that the regression model fits well.

<Example title="Example: smoking and lung cancer">

**Interpreting the scatterplot.** From the scatterplot of death rates against the number of cigarettes smoked per person, we observe a positive relationship between death rates from lung cancer ($y$) and the number of cigarettes smoked per person ($x$). As the number of cigarettes smoked increases, the death rate due to lung cancer also increases. The relationship between the number of cigarettes and death rates appears to be linear.

**Correlation coefficient.** $r = 0.69$. $r$ is positive, therefore the number of cigarettes smoked and death rates have a positive relationship. The value of $r$ also tells us the strength of the linear relationship between our two variables: there is a fairly strong positive linear relationship between the number of cigarettes smoked and death rates.

## The intercept and slope terms as random variables

We use sample data to compute $a$ and $b$. These are therefore statistics, as they are computed from a sample.

Both $a$ and $b$ are random variables, since they vary from sample to sample. They therefore both have sampling distributions, which turn out to be Normal. Can you think of a reason why they would be Normal? Hint: think of the central limit theorem.

<Solution>
Like the sample mean $\bar{x}$, both $a$ and $b$ are worked out by adding up quantities computed from every observation in the sample. The [central limit theorem](/unit/m5-sampling-distribution) tells us that sums and averages of this kind have sampling distributions that are approximately Normal.
</Solution>

The fixed, but unknown, intercept and slope population parameters could theoretically be obtained by fitting a regression model to the entire population. Since this is often not practically possible, we fit a regression line to a sample of data and use the sample statistics $a$ and $b$ to draw inferences about the unknown population parameters of interest. We write $\beta$ for the population slope.

## Regression results

The regression equation for the smoking example is

$$
\hat{y} = 6.472 + 0.529x,
$$

where $y$ is the death rate and $x$ the number of cigarettes. The coefficient of $x$ (0.529) tells us that for every one additional cigarette smoked per person, the death rate increases by 0.529 on average.

## Testing the slope

We are typically interested in focusing on the slope coefficient as evidence of a "significant" relationship between X and Y. This is based on disproving that $\beta = 0$ (i.e. no relationship between X and Y). We can also conduct tests on the intercept coefficient ($a$), but $\beta$ is the coefficient which is most informative about the relationship between X and Y.

For the smoking example:

- $H_0$: $\beta = 0$, i.e. there is no relationship between X and Y in the population.
- $H_1$: $\beta \neq 0$, i.e. there is a relationship between X and Y in the population.

$$
t = \frac{b - 0}{se(b)} = 6.306,
$$

which has $n - 2 = 44 - 2 = 42$ degrees of freedom. Both $t$ and its p-value are in Excel's output, in the **t Stat** and **P-value** columns of the slope's row: p-value $\approx 0.000$.

The p-value is overwhelmingly significant. Therefore reject $H_0$ and conclude that there is a significant linear relationship between the number of cigarettes smoked and death rates.

## More regression results

The coefficient of determination $R^2$ is 0.486. This tells us that 48.60% of the variation in death rates from lung cancer can be explained by the variation in the number of cigarettes smoked. We can (cautiously) use the regression line to make predictions.

For example, predict the death rate if the number of cigarettes smoked per person is 20:

$$
\hat{y} = 6.472 + 0.529(20) = 17.052.
$$

Note that the intercept term may be interpreted as the predicted value of $y$ when $x = 0$. For example, predict the death rate if the number of cigarettes smoked per person is 0:

$$
\hat{y} = 6.472 + 0.529(0) = 6.472.
$$

Note that interpretation of the intercept term may or may not make sense depending on the context. Suppose we fit a regression line for income ($y$) against age ($x$) and get $\hat{y} = 500 + 1000x$. Is it meaningful to interpret the intercept term in this context?

<Solution>
No. The intercept is the predicted income at age 0, and newborn babies do not earn an income of 500. The intercept is needed to place the line, but here it has no sensible meaning on its own.
</Solution>

## Caution!

A correlation coefficient close to $+1$ or $-1$, or a significant slope coefficient $b$, does not in itself prove a causal relationship.

For example, one may observe a strong positive correlation between the number of drownings at a beach and the sales figures of a nearby ice-cream vendor, but this does not imply that ice-cream sales cause an increase in drownings! Sunny weather is more likely to be the cause of the simultaneous rise in ice-cream sales and drownings (due to more beach-goers). Correlation and regression analyses can establish a relationship between two variables, but it is up to the research worker to explain the causal mechanism (usually based on theoretical and logical considerations).
